On August 3, the Governor of Texas directed the PUCT and ERCOT to audit every data center in the interconnection queue before any of them advance. The number behind the order is the one worth sitting with: ERCOT is holding roughly 474 gigawatts of requests across more than 1,800 projects. Around 90 percent are data centers. The state's record peak demand is 91,308 megawatts, set on July 22 of this year.

The queue is more than five times everything Texas has ever drawn — and the record it dwarfs is three weeks old.

The coverage that followed was loud. It usually is. New York had already halted approvals in July. PJM decided to hand the question to its member states. Three of the most consequential load-growth markets in the country moved within about a month.

What is actually happening is that grid access has become scarce, and the system is working out how to ration it. That is an ordinary thing for a market to do, even when it is disruptive to the people standing in line.

## What this actually looks like from inside

Anyone who has developed energy & technology infrastructure knows this texture. The rules change while you are mid-flight. A queue reform lands after you have already committed capital against the old timeline. A regulator asks a question nobody was required to answer last quarter. Financing assumes an energization date that just became a range.

Right now developers are re-underwriting projects that were fully modeled two months ago. Lenders are asking what a good-cause exemption does to a drawdown schedule. Hyperscalers are quietly comparing markets. Somebody's board deck from July is already wrong.

That confusion is the honest condition of this market, and it deserves to be described rather than smoothed over. But it is worth being precise about what kind of confusion it is. This is not an industry that has lost the plot. It is a mature industry absorbing a new kind of load faster than its rulebook was written to handle, and writing the rules in public while it does.

Two precisions, since they matter to anyone reading the actual order.

The first: this is a verification precondition, not a moratorium. The trades have largely called it a pause. The letter says projects must be audited before advancing, and that non-compliant projects must be denied. Those are different instruments.

The second is the one being missed almost everywhere. **The governor cannot halt grid connections by himself.** The Public Utility Commission is an independent agency, ERCOT answers to the commission, and any binding action has to come from them. That is precisely why ERCOT is *filing a request* for a good-cause exception rather than simply complying — and why August 20, when the commission takes it up, is the date that actually matters. In July, both agencies told the governor they lacked authority in key areas and asked the Legislature for more.

This is what institutional process looks like in real time. It is slower than a headline and more durable than one.

## The grid is old, and that is not an insult

There is a temptation in this moment to treat the utility system as an obstacle — a slow, defensive incumbent standing between compute and its destiny. That framing is popular and it is wrong.

The North American grid is among the largest machines ever built, engineered across a century by people solving problems that were genuinely hard, under a mandate that has always been reliability first. It was not designed for a single customer arriving with a request the size of a mid-sized country's demand, and it is not obvious that it should have been.

The other half of the fence deserves the same respect. The compute buildout is not a speculative mania visited upon a sensible industry. It is real demand, backed by real capital, chasing real economics. Both things are true, and the interesting territory is the seam between them — an old system and a new load, each entirely legitimate, meeting at an interconnection point that was never sized for the conversation.

Forge works in that seam. So do a lot of thoughtful people right now.

## Nobody knows how big this is

Here is the part that ought to make everyone more humble than the current discourse suggests.

Exelon reported that its "high probability" data center load fell nearly 40 percent in a single quarter — from 18 gigawatts to about 11. ERCOT has cautioned that its own peak demand forecast may be inflated. Developers file duplicate interconnection requests across multiple sites for a single project, and the queue counts each one.

So when we say 474 gigawatts, we do not actually know what fraction of that is a real project with real capital and a real site. Neither does ERCOT. That uncertainty is precisely what the audit exists to resolve, and it is a reasonable thing for a grid operator to want to know before rebuilding a state's transmission plan around it.

We should be comfortable saying this plainly: the most important number in the American power market right now is one nobody can currently verify. That is not a crisis. It is an early-stage market discovering the difference between interest and commitment, which every market does, usually loudly.

## What is transitory and what is not

The moratorium will lift. The audit will conclude. Batch Zero will resume in some form, probably modified, and there will be another headline. Most of what is being written this month will look small within three years.

What will not change is the underlying arithmetic. Compute demand is growing faster than transmission can be planned, permitted, and built — and transmission timelines are measured in years for reasons that are physical and procedural, not attitudinal. When a scarce resource is rationed, its price rises and substitutes appear. That is not a prediction. It is just what happens.

The substitute is already visible. Veolia was selected this month to operate a 350 megawatt behind-the-meter system powering an AI data center campus in central Ohio. The trade press is calling it a micro-grid, which it is not — at that scale it is a private generation plant with a data center attached, and the distinction matters more than the terminology suggests. What it demonstrates is real enough: utility-scale compute is choosing to sit behind the meter rather than wait in front of it. On-site generation and storage were resilience features eighteen months ago. They are becoming the way projects get energized at all.

Meanwhile the DC Circuit upheld FERC's plan to accelerate interconnection permitting, which points the same direction from the opposite side: everyone is trying to plug the same gap. Regulators, utilities, developers, and operators are all, in their own idiom and on their own timelines, working the problem. And they are doing it profitably, which is the mechanism by which it actually gets done rather than merely discussed.

## Where this leaves anyone building

Grid access has become the scarce input in digital infrastructure. Capital is abundant and silicon is allocated; a position in the queue is neither. Scarcity like that does not resolve by waiting for it to pass. People build around it, and the building is already visible.

If you own land, power, or compute, the question is what you control that does not depend on when the queue reopens.

That was worth asking last year, and it will still be worth asking after the next headline.
