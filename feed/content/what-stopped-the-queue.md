In March we published a piece arguing that interconnection throughput — not generation, not installed capacity, not decarbonization targets — was the binding constraint on the AI infrastructure cycle. The argument was that queue position had quietly become strategic inventory, and that capital would begin repricing toward certainty: existing substation headroom, brownfield sites, previously studied positions.

On August 3, the largest interconnection queue in the country stopped moving.

Enough of that call held up that it is worth examining the part that did not. The mechanism we described is not the mechanism that bit.

## What held

The repricing happened, and faster than we expected.

Senate Bill 6 now requires large-load projects in Texas to post $50,000 per megawatt in financial security, document site control, and disclose duplicative interconnection requests filed at other sites. Under the proposed rules, a developer withdrawing from the Batch Zero process forfeits 80 percent of the remaining per-megawatt balance.

That is a carrying cost and an exit penalty on a queue position. In March we wrote that interconnection rights were "evolving into strategic infrastructure inventory." They now have a price, a spread, and a cost of abandonment. Inventory is exactly the right word, and the market got there in five months.

The speculative-filing problem also proved real, and larger than we said. ERCOT is holding roughly 474 gigawatts across more than 1,800 projects against a record peak demand of 91,308 megawatts. Exelon's "high probability" data center load fell nearly 40 percent in a single quarter. ERCOT has cautioned that its own forecast may be inflated. A meaningful share of that queue is the same project counted several times.

And the strategic conclusion held. The first question in Governor Abbott's audit directive asks each project to disclose "the extent to which it is providing its own power or depending on the ERCOT grid." In March we argued for distributed deployments that reduce per-site grid burden. That is now a compliance question in the largest market in the country.

## What we got wrong

We said the constraint was study bandwidth.

Engineers, modeling cycles, cluster windows, affected-system coordination. A capacity problem, and capacity problems clear slowly and predictably. The implicit assumption was that the queue would keep moving, just badly.

The queue did not slow down. It stopped, in a day, because of a letter.

Nothing about study bandwidth changed on August 3. The engineers are the same, the modeling cycles are the same, the cluster windows are the same. What changed is that an elected official asked whether these projects should be allowed to proceed at all, and a regulator paused to answer.

We also wrote, deliberately, that "the friction is structural, not moral." That was meant generously — utilities and system operators are not failing, and the framing held them blameless for a system designed in another era. But it drew the wrong boundary. The audit examines water consumption, reliance on public subsidy, and impact on the communities where these projects site. New York halted approvals in July over substantially similar concerns. Councils in Ontario are doing the same.

Those are not engineering questions. They are questions about whether a project has earned its place, and we did not include that dimension in the model at all.

## Why the correction matters

If the constraint is study bandwidth, the strategy is to buy your way up a line: acquire positions, file early, absorb study work, wait more efficiently than the next developer.

If the constraint is political legitimacy, that strategy has a ceiling. You cannot purchase your way past a community that does not want the project, or a regulator asking who pays for the upgrade, or a governor asking how much water it draws. Queue position is worth a great deal right up until the queue itself is suspended, at which point every position in it is worth the same.

This does not invalidate bringing your own power. It changes why it works.

We argued distributed generation was faster. Speed is the least of it.

A project earns its place by being worth having nearby. That is a question about the thing itself, not about how it is presented — how it is built, what it draws, what it emits, what it sounds like at the fence line, what condition it leaves the site in twenty years from now. Green building standards, environmental performance, water discipline, and genuine smallness are not concessions offered to smooth a permit. They are the substance of whether a community should want the project at all.

Scale is where most of this is decided, and it is where the language has gotten loose. A 250 megawatt installation described as a micro-grid is not one. A true micro-grid is ten megawatts or less — small enough to sit inside an existing industrial envelope, to island cleanly, to be maintained by a local crew, and to be genuinely unobtrusive rather than merely permitted. Below that threshold a different set of things becomes possible, in the near term and across the full life of the asset.

The capacity still gets built. It arrives as a network of small units rather than one large one, which means a hundred megawatts of compute never has to land on any single community as a hundred megawatt imposition. That is an architectural choice, and it has to be made early — it is not something a large project can retrofit once the objections start.

What the last five months clarified is that the constraint has two faces. One is engineering and we described it. The other is whether a project deserves to exist where it is proposed, and that question is now being asked out loud in three major markets within about a month.

## What we still do not know

The honest list is longer than the confident one.

We do not know how much of the 474 gigawatts is real. Neither does ERCOT, which is the entire purpose of the audit.

We do not know how long the pause lasts. The Public Utility Commission takes up ERCOT's request for a good-cause exception on August 20. Worth noting, since much of the coverage has missed it: the governor cannot suspend interconnections by himself. The commission is independent and ERCOT answers to it, which is why ERCOT is filing a request rather than simply complying. If the audit runs into the 2027 legislative session, the framework itself may be rewritten.

We do not know whether this becomes the national pattern or stays a Texas story with New York rhyming alongside it.

And we do not know whether the audit purges the queue or merely delays it. Those produce very different markets. A cleaned queue is a smaller, more credible pipeline where remaining positions are worth more. A delayed queue is the same congestion, five months older.

## Where that leaves the thesis

The constraint we identified in March is still the constraint. We described its engineering face and missed the other one, which is whether a project has earned the ground it stands on.

The practical conclusion survives the correction and comes out sharper. Control what does not depend on permission you have not been granted, and build the kind of thing a community has no reason to refuse. That was worth doing when the argument was speed alone.

We will revisit this after August 20, and some of what is written above will need correcting too. In the meantime there are three questions worth sitting with.

How might a site be built so that the question of whether it belongs never has to be litigated?

What would make a community ask for one of these, rather than agree to tolerate it?

And what condition should the land be in on the day the equipment comes out?
